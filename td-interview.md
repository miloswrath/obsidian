## STORIES
---
I've been building things with code since I was a kid — it started as curiosity and turned into a career direction pretty quickly once I realized software could actually change how people experience the world. That thread runs through everything I've done since: at the Brain Cognition Lab I built agentic AI tooling and a full-stack reporting platform that 30+ researchers depend on daily, and at Cerebra I architected a clinical communication platform in a HIPAA environment that measurably improved patient outcomes. In both cases I was the person taking something from zero to production — not prototyping, actually shipping.

What draws me to this role specifically is the scale at which TD SYNNEX operates. Most AI work I see stays contained — one team, one product, one organization. TD SYNNEX sits at a point in the industry where the AI solutions built here get distributed across the entire partner channel. That means the work has a multiplier effect that almost nothing else at this stage of a career can offer. I want to build things that matter at scale, and the AI Pioneers program is one of the few intern experiences where that's actually possible — where the output isn't a demo that gets shelved, it's something that goes in front of real partners with real business stakes.

I'm finishing dual degrees at Iowa — HCI and Health Informatics — which means I think about AI not just as a technical system but as something that has to work for the humans on the other end of it. I think that combination is genuinely useful for building AI products people actually adopt, and it's what I want to keep developing.

**Strengths**

Lead with full-stack ownership since it's your most differentiated quality for this role. Most AI interns can train a model or build a frontend — fewer have done both end-to-end in a production environment with real stakes. Frame it like this:

_"My biggest strength is end-to-end ownership. At Cerebra I took a clinical platform from database schema to production deployment in a HIPAA environment — I wasn't handed off pieces of the problem, I was responsible for the whole thing. I think that's rare, and it means I don't get stuck at boundaries between systems or teams."_

For AI/ML in production, the key is emphasizing production specifically — not research, not notebooks, not demos:

_"I've shipped ML models into real user-facing products twice now — Bayesian risk models at Cerebra, and agentic AI tooling at the Brain Lab. Both had non-technical people depending on the output daily. That distinction between research AI and production AI matters a lot to me."_

For translating technical work, tie it directly to your HCI background — it gives it a credential:

_"My HCI degree is essentially a degree in understanding how technical systems meet human behavior. At the Brain Lab I built tooling specifically so non-technical researchers could query complex neuroimaging data in plain language. I think about the person on the other end of what I build, not just the system."_

---

**Weakness**

The strongest real-but-not-disqualifying weakness from your background is this: **you've mostly worked in environments where you had significant autonomy and context** — you designed the systems, you knew the data, you set the standards. The weakness is that working inside a larger pre-existing codebase or under someone else's architectural decisions is less familiar territory.

Frame it like this:

_"Honestly, most of my experience has been in environments where I had a lot of ownership and context from the start — I built the systems, so I understood them deeply. The flip side of that is I have less experience parachuting into a large existing codebase and getting productive quickly. It's something I've been actively working on — I've been doing more open source contribution and code review on projects I didn't build — but I'd name it as an area I'm still developing."_

Why this works: it's credible given your resume, it doesn't threaten your core value to the role, and the self-awareness plus the concrete fix ("open source contribution, code review") shows maturity. It also won't surprise them — they already know you've been building greenfield projects in research labs.
