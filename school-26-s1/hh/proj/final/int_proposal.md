Reminiscence therapy is usually defined as **structured discussion of past experiences, often in groups, using prompts like photos, music, objects, or timelines to stimulate autobiographical memory and conversation**. It’s grounded in **Butler’s life review** and **Erikson’s life-stage theory**, and is widely used in dementia care to support person-centred care and personhood. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6494367/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Reminiscence therapy for dementia - PMC - PubMed Central"))

Below are **humanities-type gaps** (not neural/mechanistic ones), then an example intervention.

---

## Humanities-oriented gaps in reminiscence therapy

### Gap 1 – Narrative agency: _whose_ story is it, really?

Recent narrative analyses and reviews show RT is often:

- Delivered in highly structured, staff-led formats with preset themes and memory triggers. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8132012/ "Towards an increased understanding of reminiscence therapy for people with dementia: A narrative analysis - PMC"))
    
- Inconsistently grounded in explicit theory about personhood, identity, or narrative ethics. ([PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8132012/ "Towards an increased understanding of reminiscence therapy for people with dementia: A narrative analysis - PMC"))
    
- Poor at reporting how much **control older adults actually have** over what gets told, recorded, or used in care.
    

Work on life story and user involvement in dementia care notes that:

- Life story work is frequently **organisationally driven** (forms, templates) rather than resident-driven, and staff sometimes treat it as a task rather than a genuine listening practice. ([Essex Open Access Research Repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/25601/1/1207613-PhD%20Thesis.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The use of life story work in an age inclusive dementia service"))
    
- Qualitative studies show that when done well, it can transform how staff “see” the person, but we know little about how residents experience **power, consent, and ownership** of their stories. ([White Rose eTheses Online](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/14996/1/554913_Vol1.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "\"It made me see him in a different light.\" The use of life story ..."))
    

**Humanities gap:** We lack good evidence on _narrative agency_—how reminiscence changes who gets to define the person’s story, how “messy” or painful stories are handled, and whether residents feel truly heard vs. curated.

---

### Gap 2 – Cultural and linguistic justice

Umbrella and systematic reviews point out that:

- Most RT trials are with **white, Western, institutionalised** older adults. ([SpringerLink](https://bmcgeriatr.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12877-025-06484-6?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Effectiveness of reminiscence therapy on multiple health ..."))
    
- Newer work is starting to explore culturally adapted RT (e.g., Spanish- and Vietnamese-speaking elders; older adults living with HIV in Taiwan), but these are still the exception. ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1669119/full?utm_source=chatgpt.com "A randomized controlled trial study of a life review art ..."))
    

From a humanities perspective, we don’t yet know:

- How cultural norms about storytelling, family, trauma, and respect shape what feels safe or meaningful to recall.
    
- How RT might inadvertently **flatten** complex identities (e.g., racialised, queer, migrant histories) into “nice, shareable” stories that are comfortable for staff.
    

**Humanities gap:** We need work that treats reminiscence as **culturally situated narrative practice**, not just a generic positive-psychology exercise.

---

### Gap 3 – From one-off project to care culture

Life story and digital storytelling studies suggest:

- Digital life storybooks and RT can support identity and relationships, but **how they are embedded into everyday routines** is under-described. ([Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17482631.2021.1920349?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Supporting identity and relationships amongst people with ..."))
    
- Participatory action research in dementia services shows that organisational culture and staff training heavily determine whether life stories actually influence care planning or remain “nice projects in a folder.” ([Essex Open Access Research Repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/25601/1/1207613-PhD%20Thesis.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com "The use of life story work in an age inclusive dementia service"))
    

**Humanities gap:** We lack longitudinal, qualitative work on **institutions as storytelling environments**—how RT reshapes the narratives circulating among staff, families, and residents.

## Example intervention to address Gaps 1 & 2

_(Culturally grounded, agency-focused reminiscence)_

### Title

**“My Story, In My Words” (MSIMW): Co-created narrative circles for culturally diverse residents with dementia**

### Population / setting

- Older adults with mild–moderate dementia in a **long-term care facility**,
    
- Oversampling residents from **minoritised cultural/linguistic groups** (e.g., Spanish-speaking Latinx elders),
    
- Family members and frontline staff invited as collaborators.
    

### Core design (humanities-centered)

1. **Co-design phase (2–3 workshops):**
    
    - Residents (as able), families, and staff choose **themes, languages, and materials** (music, foods, religious objects, photos) that feel authentic.
        
    - Establish explicit **story rules**: right to pass, to stop, to exclude topics or people; shared agreements on confidentiality and respect.
        
2. **Narrative circles (8 weekly sessions):**
    
    - Small groups (4–6 residents) led by a facilitator trained in **narrative practice and trauma-informed care**, plus a bilingual co-facilitator where needed.
        
    - Each session centres one resident as “story host,” who chooses the prompt (e.g., “my first job,” “migration,” “a time I felt proud”).
        
    - Stories are captured as **short “story tiles”** (photo + 2–3 sentences in the resident’s preferred language), which the resident can revise or veto at any point.
        
3. **Story integration phase (4 weeks):**
    
    - Story tiles are compiled into a **personal story booklet** and a **staff-facing care summary** written in narrative form (“What matters to me,” “How to comfort me when I’m distressed”).
        
    - Staff reflection huddles discuss one resident’s story each week and how it should concretely change care routines (e.g., music choices, meal conversation, holiday activities).

## Proposed outcomes

### Primary outcomes (resident-centred, humanities-aligned)

1. **Narrative agency / feeling known**
    
    - Change from baseline to post-intervention in a composite outcome, e.g.:
        
        - Resident-reported or proxy-rated **“feeling heard / understood as a person”** (Likert items developed from qualitative work).
            
        - Qualitative coding of interviews for themes like “voice,” “choice,” and “ownership” over the story.
            
2. **Cultural resonance of care**
    
    - Proportion of care-plan notes and daily activities that explicitly reference **resident’s cultural values, language, or key life themes** documented in their story materials.
        

### Secondary outcomes

1. **Resident outcomes**
    
    - Depressive symptoms (e.g., Geriatric Depression Scale or Cornell Scale).
        
    - Quality of life (e.g., QoL-AD).
        
    - Observed engagement and affect during everyday interactions, coded from short video samples.
        
2. **Staff and family outcomes**
    
    - Staff **person-centred attitudes** and perceived closeness to residents (validated scales + interviews). ([Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17482631.2021.1920349?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Supporting identity and relationships amongst people with ..."))
        
    - Family perceptions of whether their relative’s **“real self”** is recognised and respected in the facility (qualitative interviews, simple rating scales).
        
3. **Process outcomes**
    
    - Feasibility: recruitment/retention, session attendance.
        
    - Fidelity: proportion of sessions where residents chose themes/materials; instances where residents exercised the right to stop or veto story use.
        
    - Documentation: how often story materials are accessed/mentioned in clinical notes or handovers.

## Research Question
---
_How can reminiscence therapy be adapted to enhance both narrative agency and cultural-linguistic authenticity for diverse older adults in long-term care, ensuring they retain ownership over their autobiographical stories and can express memories that reflect their cultural identities and lived experiences?_
