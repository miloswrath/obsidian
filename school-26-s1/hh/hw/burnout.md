During my sophomore spring semester, burnout became a constant presence in my life. I was working twenty hours a week in a research lab while taking eighteen semester hours, and the combination created a cycle of deadlines, exams, and lab responsibilities. My days were a blur of tasks that rarely connected to each other, forcing me to jump back and forth between projects, assignments, and study sessions with no real pause. Inside my head it felt almost manic, like my thoughts were moving too quickly from one obligation to the next without rest. I often worked twelve to sixteen hours a day, every day, trying to keep up.

The first thing to disappear was my social life. I pulled back from friends almost without noticing, and weekends became a time to crash, sometimes sleeping twelve to 16 hours just to recover enough to start again. Eventually I tried to take control with extreme time-blocking, planning my days down to fifteen-minute intervals. It helped me stay on top of everything, but it also felt obsessive—like I was living in a padded room. The schedule eased the chaos, but it also highlighted how unnatural my routine was.

Looking ahead, I hope that once I move into a full-time job I’ll be able to focus on one thing at a time instead of juggling twenty different priorities. I imagine a life where work stays at work, where evenings and weekends leave space for friends or hobbies or the experiences I’ve been putting off. The thought of slowing down, of having the freedom to be social and to choose what matters outside of constant deadlines, feels like a godsend after years of running on caffeine and adrenaline. Burnout has taught me that productivity without balance is unsustainable, and hopefully my future is about finding a way to thrive without losing myself in the process.
